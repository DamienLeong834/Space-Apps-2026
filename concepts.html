The brief in one line: create an engaging, story-driven educational tool that teaches school-age and casual space enthusiasts about the hardware NASA left on the Moon, Mars and elsewhere — showing how the missions operated, not just listing facts, and drawing a clear line between the engineering and the science it produced. Accuracy is graded.
Concept 01

Ghost Map

Interactive web experience

An explorable map of the Moon, Mars and deep space. Click glowing beacons to open a story drawer for each abandoned machine — what it was sent to do, what it found, and whether it is still working. Three switchable worlds, live-vs-silent legend, and a card grid below.

Chapter Zero · STORY

PATCHWORK

Seven-beat comic × live story hook

The story behind the map. A salvage robot ordered to clear NASA's abandoned hardware cannot bring itself to throw any of it away, so it builds a companion — Patch — out of the junk. Seven comic panels with friendly binocular-eyed characters, a seven-line story board, and a full prose telling. Answers typed into the atlas are handed to Patch and become its first original sentence, so every reader gets a different ending.

How to choose

There is no single winner — the right pick depends on your team's skills and how much time you have before submission.

Live main experience · fusion

Concept 06 — The Ghost Atlas

Now the site's landing page. Merges the spatial beacon map (01), halftone comic panels (02) and the typed rarity-descent engine (05) into one evidence-driven learning loop.

  • Live now: open index.html or the site root
  • Why: hits narrative, accuracy, accessibility and “wow factor” in one build
  • Risk: most complex of the five — keep it to 10 questions and 7 chapters
Fastest route

Concept 01 — Ghost Map

Fastest route to something impressive, easiest to extend, and it hits the “accuracy” and “accessibility” criteria with no extra work.

  • Best if: you have 1–2 days, or a team with mixed skill levels
  • Then add: a story chapter or audio layer from concepts 02/03
  • Risk: weakest pure narrative — compensate with richer story text
Highest ceiling

Concept 02 — Graphic Novel

The most memorable answer to a storytelling challenge. Judges remember a story; they rarely remember a data table.

  • Best if: someone on your team can draw or design
  • Then add: the map as a “where did this happen?” appendix
  • Risk: takes the longest; scope it to 1 story, not 6
Safest hybrid

Concept 04 — Silent Fleet

Big visual impact, covers the most hardware, and the comparison table makes the science easy to judge quickly.

  • Best if: you want breadth over depth
  • Then add: narrative captions per node from concept 02
  • Risk: can feel like a list unless each node tells a real story

What to do next

Whatever you pick, these five steps apply. Run them in order.

  1. Pick a format and a target age groupThese two decisions drive everything else. “School-age” is broad — commit to something like ages 10–14 and write to it.
  2. Choose your hardware — 4 to 6 pieces maximumDepth beats breadth. Four machines with real stories beat fifteen with blurbs. The templates above already contain a vetted set to draw from.
  3. Wait for the Resources tab to open — Friday, November 13The official datasets are not published yet. Re-check then and align your numbers to what NASA provides.
  4. Verify every fact against a primary sourceAccuracy is explicitly graded. Keep a spreadsheet of claim → source URL for each figure you publish.
  5. Make the engineering → science link explicitFor every machine, answer: what was it built to do, what did it find, and why does that discovery matter to someone on Earth?
