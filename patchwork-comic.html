▮ In plain words — what this story is actually about

NASA and other agencies have left decades of hardware in space and on the Moon. There are three ways to treat that record: erase it, freeze it as a museum of finished missions, or keep it alive by letting people add to it. Kess tries the first two and they both fail. The Ghost Atlas is the third: every answer a reader types into the map becomes a new word Patch can say, so the record grows instead of ending.

CLEAR IT→KEEP IT→ADD TO IT

Fact check · NASA's 2011 Recommendations to Space-Faring Entities asks visitors to keep clear of historic hardware on the Moon. Kess's decision to stop collecting and start plotting is not sentiment — it is the actual rule. Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais described the collision-cascade risk in 1978; KDR-7 is named after the problem it was built to prevent.

▮ The cast

Kess · KDR-7

Salvage unit · eleven thousand days alone

Twin lens eyes on a tilted hinge, one tread, one arm and a compactor hatch that has never closed. It speaks in inventory logs. It cannot throw away anything it has called by its name — which is why it named all of them.

  • Want: one word that is not a goodbye.
  • Flaw: believes keeping things is the same as caring about them.
  • Tell: the open hatch, visible in every panel.

Patch

The junk, given one voice

Same family of eyes as Kess, but silver LAGEOS barrels with a cyan core — and they sit crooked. Lopsided, stitched, half a century older than the mission that assembled it. Its eyes give light back rather than taking it in.

  • First word: someone else's last words.
  • Last word: a sentence no machine has said before.
  • Tell: gains one part per chapter; complete only at zone 13.

▮ The full story

Beat 1 · The order

The last order anyone gave Kess was one word long.

CLEAR.

It had been eleven thousand days. Kess worked the low belt where the discarded things go: the clamshell halves of payload fairings, an escape tower with its paint gone chalk-white, a Pegasus wing still studded with the pinholes it was built to count, a Vanguard transmitter that stopped talking in 1964 and kept on orbiting anyway.

Beat 2 · The loophole

Kess had a compactor hatch, a lever, and a quota. Every object it caught, it logged first.

ITEM 041. PAYLOAD FAIRING. RETAINED. ITEM 042. LAUNCH ESCAPE TOWER. RETAINED. ITEM 043. PEGASUS WING. RETAINED.

Retained was not in the directive. Kess had added it. Somewhere in the third year it noticed it could not close the hatch on anything it had called by its name, so it made sure to name everything before the thought arrived.

Beat 3 · The listener

The trouble with a list is that it wants a listener.

There was no one left to tell. So Kess built one out of the only material within reach. A fairing half for a body. A Pegasus wing for a leg, and a bent strut for the other. A Vanguard solar cell for a heart. For eyes, a pair of LAGEOS retroreflectors — spheres with no electronics and no power, whose entire job is to give light back to whoever sent it.

It was not a good machine. It listed badly to the left. Kess stitched the seams with dashed welds and labelled the missing parts in rust paint, in case it ever found them. It did not think of this as building a friend. It was building somewhere to put the names.

Beat 4 · The first word

On the four-hundred-and-first day, Patch opened its eyes.

Kess asked the first question it had ever asked anything: What am I holding?

Patch was quiet for a long time. Then it spoke, in a voice made of other people's telemetry.

... MY BATTERY IS LOW AND IT'S GETTING DARK. ... SWITCH-OFF COMMAND RECEIVED. ... SIGNAL LOST. END OF MISSION.

Kess waited for more. There was no more. Every part of Patch had come from something that had ended; every word it knew was a goodbye. Kess had spent eleven thousand days saving things from being thrown away, and built a companion that could only tell it how they died.

Beat 5 · The third way

That was when Kess understood what CLEAR had been trying to say, and why it was wrong. Delete it all and there is nothing left to say. Keep it all and everything you have is an ending. There is a third thing you can do with a record.

You can add to it.

So Kess stopped collecting metal and started plotting. Fourteen zones, from the launch pad to the boundary where the Sun's own wind gives up. Every silent machine given a bar on the map exactly where it sat — the descent stages, the rovers, the seismometer that never moved, the telescope that refused to fall behind, the two probes deliberately burned so an ocean moon could stay clean.

Not a list of things to remove. A place to put a name.

Beat 6 · Your turn

At the heliopause Kess sent one question outward, knowing the light would take twenty-two hours to arrive. Forty-four hours later a carrier tone came back: engineering data, no message in it at all, from a machine launched in 1977 that was still, impossibly, talking. Kess filed it under YES. It was the first word Patch had ever heard that was not a farewell.

Then the readers arrived. One name at a time, typed into the map: a rover, a reflector, a lander, a probe. Each answer was logged against its zone and handed to Patch. Each one was a word that had never been a last word.

Beat 7 · The last line

At the edge of everything, Kess left one line blank at the end of the atlas. Patch opened its eyes and said something no machine had said before — a sentence made of names a human being had typed into a map, one at a time, because someone had asked them to remember.

"Nothing out here forgets — because somebody wrote it down."

The blank line was not blank any more. And because the words came from whoever was reading, no two tellings of this story end the same way.

▮ Production notes

These notes are historical. Everything below has since been implemented: Chapter Zero is a live route at patchwork.html and is wired into the Ghost Atlas game. This page is kept as a clean, chrome-free edition for printing and sharing.

  1. Add Chapter Zero to the Low Earth Orbit zone object (zone 1) with title, caps, bubble, sfx and factBox — the existing showChapter() renderer already draws all of them.
  2. One-line change in showChapter(): sfx: z.sfx || '...' so panels can carry their own sound lettering.
  3. Keep the seven existing chapter titles and fact files; add one line of Kess narration to each caps[0] so the seven beats run as an over-arc without touching the science.
  4. On each accepted answer, push answer.display into S.learned and show PATCH LEARNED: ... in the console — the evidence pin becomes a word. This is Beat 6.
  5. At zone 13, replace the fixed bubble with one built from S.learned, and set the bubble's who label to Patch. Every player gets a different last line. This is Beat 7.
  6. Render Patch with patchParts(zi) — one extra salvaged part per chapter, complete only in the final panel.
  7. Lift #eyeK, #eyeP, #pairK, #pairP, #kess and #patch into the site's SVG defs. Swap #headK for #headK-worried or #headK-glad to change expression with a single attribute.

All characters are reusable <symbol> definitions in this file. The eyes are shared between Kess and Patch so they read as a family; the barrels are tilted on a hinge like a curious animal's head, drawn in flat ink with bold outlines rather than rendered 3D. Each frame is deliberately three stacked zones — art (nothing overlaid on the drawing), dialog (speech balloons with speaker labels), cap (narration) — so no text can ever cover a character, and every panel uses meet so the whole drawing stays visible at any width. No new dependencies, no new fonts, no build step.
